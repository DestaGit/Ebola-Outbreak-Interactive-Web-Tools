Why was HCW mortality so high? · Three systemic factors
🧤
PPE Shortages & Supply Chain Failure
By mid-2014 the outbreak scale far exceeded available Personal Protective Equipment across Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone. Healthcare workers treated actively infectious patients with improvised or inadequate protection — thin gloves, no sealed masks, repurposed raincoats. MSF reported critical PPE gaps in July 2014, weeks before the WHO PHEIC.
Peak HCW infection month: Oct 2014 · 185 new HCW cases in 30 days
🔬
Repeated High Viral Load Exposure
Healthcare workers had sustained, repeated contact with patients at their most infectious stage — during bleeding, vomiting, and diarrhoea. Unlike a single community-level exposure, a nurse treating multiple Ebola patients in a shift faced cumulative viral load far exceeding a single household exposure. Needle-stick injuries and contaminated surfaces compounded risk significantly.
Kenema Government Hospital, Sierra Leone: most HCW deaths of any single facility
🏥
Pre-existing Healthcare System Fragility
Liberia had 0.01 physicians per 1,000 people before the outbreak. Sierra Leone had 0.02. Guinea had 0.10. As HCWs fell ill and died, remaining staff faced unsustainable patient loads with inadequate training in Ebola-specific infection control. Each HCW death removed a multiplier — a single doctor or nurse whose absence meant dozens more patients went untreated.
Liberia physician density: 1 per 100,000 people at outbreak start
